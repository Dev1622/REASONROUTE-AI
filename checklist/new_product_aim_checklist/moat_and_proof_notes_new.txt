MOAT AND PROOF NOTES

How we arrived at the thesis:
- Dispatchers are the final gatekeepers before routes reach drivers.
- In current systems, they review routes and often manually override by changing assignment or stop sequence.
- They usually do not redraw turn-by-turn driving paths.
- They operate under real time pressure, so the decision moment is rarely a deep analysis exercise.
- Their working priority is usually:
  keep the route moving,
  protect ETA/SLA,
  get the truck out the door.
- Once they override, the system typically just accepts the forced sequence and recalculates ETA and mileage.
- The software does not create a real decision layer around that moment:
  it does not clearly explain the route logic,
  it does not show explicit cost tradeoff visibility in the override moment,
  and it does not help compare whether the original route was actually better.

This is the core workflow truth:
- Black box route recommendation
- Fast dispatcher review under time pressure
- Manual drag-and-drop override when something feels wrong or risky
- No explicit cost visibility in the actual override decision moment
- No structured knowledge capture
- No credible middle layer between:
  keep the current route,
  manually override it,
  or evaluate a better alternative

That creates the real Trust-Profit Gap:
- Dispatchers protect service outcomes using intuition and speed.
- The business loses margin because the cost of those changes is hidden or not salient in the moment.
- The dispatcher's override is not guaranteed to be best.
- The original route is not guaranteed to be wrong.
- A better third option may exist, but current tools do not make it easy to discover or evaluate.

The sharper product truth:
- The gap is not simply that manual review exists.
- The gap is that dispatchers are forced into a false binary under time pressure.
- They must either trust the proposed route or override it quickly without a strong way to compare:
  the original route,
  the manual override,
  and a better negotiated alternative.

Core proof-backed product claims we can stand behind:
- Legacy routing workflows are generally review-and-approve, not full blind automation.
- Manual overrides commonly happen through assignment and sequencing.
- Existing software usually recalculates the locked route rather than negotiating the decision with the dispatcher.
- Dispatchers often make these choices in an ETA-first operating context, not a financial-analysis context.
- The middle layer between "accept" and "override" is underbuilt.

Claims that are strong as thesis but still need careful wording:
- "No major player explains routes in a dispatcher-facing reasoning UI."
- "We are the first Glass Box."
- "Every override widens the moat."
- "Manual override behavior directly causes X% cost leakage in all fleets."

How to speak about them:
- Use them as positioning statements, not unqualified market facts.
- Prefer language like:
  - "Current tools largely act like black boxes in the dispatcher decision moment"
  - "We have not seen commercial dispatcher-first reasoning UIs become standard"
  - "Our moat grows as dispatcher corrections become structured training data"
  - "Current tools do not make it easy to compare the proposed route, the manual override, and a better negotiated alternative"

The moat, clearly:
1. Tribal Knowledge Data Moat
2. Workflow Integration Moat
3. Trust-First Brand Moat
4. Human Feedback Flywheel

Best YC-style phrasing:
- Competitors are building robots to replace the dispatcher.
- We are building an exoskeleton to empower them.
- Our moat is not the code.
- Our moat is the proprietary dataset of human logistics intuition captured through negotiated route decisions.

Best product-gap phrasing:
- ReasonRoute does not assume the dispatcher is wrong.
- ReasonRoute does not assume the original route is right.
- ReasonRoute creates the missing decision layer that helps the dispatcher evaluate:
  1. keep the current route
  2. accept the manual adjustment
  3. generate a better negotiated alternative
